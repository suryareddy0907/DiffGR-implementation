Abstract. Generative models have recently been used to produce per-
sonalized recommendations by simulating user preferences in continuous
space. However, existing work often overlooks crucial discrete structural
relationships, such as graph sparsity and user-item topology, reducing
recommendation accuracy. Current graph diﬀusion models that manipu-
late the full adjacency matrix in continuous space face scalability issues
and poorly structured intermediates adjacency matrix. To address these
problems, we develop DiﬀGR, a novel discrete diﬀusion model for person-
alised recommendation. DiﬀGR operates in discrete space, manipulating
individual user-item bipartite sub-graphs by randomly removing edges
in forward diﬀusion and restoring them based on a Bernoulli distribution
in reverse, preserving graph structure and avoiding full adjacency matrix
loading. Empirical studies show that DiﬀGR achieves competitive per-
sonalised recommendation results and generates synthetic graphs with
high ﬁdelity to the original dataset, suggesting a promising direction for
discrete diﬀusion modelling in recommender systems.

Introduction
Generative models including Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) [20] and
Variational Auto-Encoders (VAEs) [13] have demonstrated eﬀectiveness in rec-
ommender systems (RSs) [21]. Generative recommenders are trained to predict
the likelihood of user interactions with items they have not engaged with by
learning the underlying generative process [20]. Recently, diﬀusion models (DMs)
have gained more attention as state-of-the-art generative models in RS [21].
Limited research has explored the usage of DMs in RS. DMs [11,12,19,21]
create personalised recommendations by gradually injecting noise into user pro-
ﬁles to simulate changes in interest and then eliminating the noise to align the
new interests with the original preferences. However, current DMs in RS face

Most RS data possesses an inherently graphical structure [23,24]. Framing
recommendation as a graph-based task eﬀectively captures and uses the struc-
tural information that encompasses complex relationships between users and
items within datasets [4]. Existing DMs [2,15,18] used for graph reconstruction

rendering them impractical for large-scale datasets commonly used in RSs.
To overcome these limitations, we propose DiﬀGR, the ﬁrst diﬀusion-based
recommender that operates the diﬀusion process directly on RS graphs in dis-
crete space. DiﬀGR models the interactions of each user with items as a bipartite
sub-graph, avoiding the computational need for the full adjacency matrix. Dur-
ing forward diﬀusion, edges are randomly removed in each sub-graph, and in the
reverse process, they are restored following the Bernoulli distribution. By directly
manipulating the graph in discrete space, DiﬀGR preserves the graph’s structure,
making it well-suited to large RS datasets. We conduct extensive experiments on
three widely used datasets, comparing DiﬀGR with multiple competitive base-
lines. DiﬀGR achieves superior performance in personalized recommendation,
demonstrating an eﬀective use of discrete diﬀusion models in RSs. Its ability
to generate high-ﬁdelity synthetic graphs makes it a powerful tool for future
research on generative RS models.

Discrete Diﬀusion
To preserve graph topology and capture user-item structural relationships, Dif-
fGR is inspired by [2] and leverages the generation ability of diﬀusion models in
discrete space for better personalised recommendations. Figure 1a illustrates the
diﬀusion process of DiﬀGR. Each user’s item interactions form a bipartite sub-
graph. The forward process randomly removes edges using Bernoulli noise until
the graph is empty, while the reverse incrementally reconstructs it in discrete

2.2
Personalised Recommendation and Graph Generation
Our denoising model has four modules (Fig. 1b), reconstructing the edges
through its approximation ˆG0 given the noise input Gt. An encoder ﬁrst learns the
latent representation embGt of Gt. As the diﬀusion works on individual user-item
sub-graphs, mutual information across sub-graphs is hard to capture. To counter
this, we use an ID embedding module projecting node IDs into embeddings (e.g.,
EmbU and EmbI for user and item nodes respectively). For a sub-graph G,
the user embedding merger concatenates embu, embGt and time step embed-
ding embt, producing embfinalu. We encode embt using Sinusoidal positional
embedding [3]. Similarly, the entire item node embeddings EmbfinalI are pro-
duced. Edge embeddings Embe are formed via the Hadamard product between
embfinalu and EmbfinalI and sent to the edge prediction module to predict each
edge’s Bernoulli probability ˆe0
i of ˆG0. The ID embedding module injects mutual
information across diﬀerent bipartite graphs, enriching edge embeddings.

model has already learnt the personalised information. At time step t = 0, each

We conduct experiments on three widely used datasets: 1) ML-1M1 (movie
ratings with 22.4M edges), 2) Pinterest [6] (user-pinned images with 0.53B
edges), and 3) Yelp2018 [22] (restaurant reviews with 1.2B edges). User inter-
actions are randomly split into 70% train, 10% validation, and 20% test sets. To
preserve real user behavior, we use minimal pre-processing [5,17], excluding only
users with fewer than ﬁve interactions for suﬃcient training data and accurate
graph reconstruction in reverse diﬀusion.
We compare DiﬀGR with six competitive and well-known baselines, cover-
ing: pairwise learning to rank BPR [16], light graph convolution LightGCN
[7], generative VAE Mult-VAE [13] and continuous denoising diﬀusion-based
recommenders CODIGEM [19], DiﬀRec [21], and L-DiﬀRec [21].
Parameters for all approaches are tuned following [21], except for L-DiﬀRec.
For L-DiﬀRec, we set item clusters to 1, use default VAE settings, and tune other
parameters the same as DiﬀRec. For DiﬀGR, we tune the cosine noise scheduler
oﬀset s from {0.8, 0.08, 0.008} and select dropout ratio in {0.1, 0.3, 0.5}. Diﬀusion
steps are set to T = 5, and embedding mergers and encoder structures are ﬁxed
for eﬃciency. All models are trained with learning rates from {1e−2, 1e−3, 1e−4}.

4
Experiment Results
Table 1 reports each algorithm’s recommendation performance by dataset. Dif-
fGR outperforms both generative and non-generative models across all datasets,
demonstrating that it beneﬁts from modelling the complex structural of user-
item graphs through the diﬀusion process in discrete space. It shows stronger
gains on sparser datasets (e.g., Pinterest (sparsity: 99.73%) and Yelp2018
(99.98%) compared to ML-1M (95.53%)), where traditional and continuous diﬀu-
sion models may struggle with complex, implicit user preference. DiﬀGR handles
sparse graphs at each time step and is naturally more capable of learning from
extremely sparse datasets, demonstrating the advantages of discrete diﬀusion
models in RSs over other models.
To further analyse the synthetic graph generated by DiﬀGR, we compare its
hub-like structure and power-law distribution to the original graph using (ρ) [26]
and (PLE) [1], respectively. The original and generated user and item nodes are
ranked by edge count in both graphs, with ρ measuring the correlation between
these rankings. As shown in Table 2, ρ values for users and items are close to
1 (mostly > 0.9), indicating that nodes with higher edge counts in the original
graph retain them in the generated graph. This preserves node positioning sim-
ilarity, with hub-center nodes remaining central. The PLE indicates how node
degree frequencies in the synthetic graph follow the original graph’s power-law
distribution. Table 2 shows low PLE values (around 0.05, except for Pinterest),
suggesting that the generated graph closely preserves this distribution. Figure 2
shows the distribution of embeddings learned by LightGCN of original and gen-
erated users. Both distributions are similar, with only minor angular shifts due
to diﬀusion process uncertainty.

5
Conclusion and Future Work
In this paper, we present DiﬀGR, the ﬁrst discrete diﬀusion-based generative
RS. DiﬀGR focuses on modelling the crucial structural relationships between
users and items in a discrete space by diﬀusing user-item graphs. Moreover, it
avoids the need for loading entire adjacency matrices by directly manipulating
each user’s interaction graphs and we show the generated graphs match the
properties of the original. The experimental results show that DiﬀGR achieves
state of the art performance. We expect DiﬀGR’s ability to generate synthetic
graphs will stimulate interesting new directions in generative modelling for RSs.
